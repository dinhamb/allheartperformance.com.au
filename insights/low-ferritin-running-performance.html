Low Ferritin and Running Performance: What Does the Evidence Actually Show?

Running & Physiology  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Iron deficiency can affect endurance performance even without anaemia. But raising ferritin does not necessarily make a runner faster.

Persistent fatigue, unusually difficult sessions and declining performance can prompt runners to question whether their iron stores are low. For female runners, menstrual blood loss, dietary factors and endurance-training demands can increase the risk of deficiency.

Iron is essential for oxygen transport and aerobic energy production. It is therefore reasonable to expect that restoring depleted stores will improve performance. Yet controlled studies are mixed: some show endurance benefits, while others show improved blood markers without better exercise performance.

The important distinction is between correcting a deficiency and assuming that more iron means better performance.

1. Why iron matters for endurance running

Iron is a core component of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in blood. It is also present in myoglobin and proteins involved in mitochondrial energy production. These roles matter in endurance exercise, which relies heavily on aerobic metabolism.

If deficiency progresses to iron-deficiency anaemia, reduced haemoglobin can impair oxygen-carrying capacity. But depleted iron stores can occur before haemoglobin becomes abnormally low. Some experimental evidence suggests this earlier stage can affect exercise or training adaptation, although not consistently in every athlete. [1] [2]

2. Ferritin and haemoglobin measure different things

Ferritin is an indicator of stored iron; haemoglobin helps assess whether deficiency has progressed to anaemia. Runners can have depleted stores while haemoglobin remains within a normal reference range: iron deficiency without anaemia.

Ferritin does not reveal exactly how much endurance performance is affected. Inflammation and strenuous exercise can influence interpretation. Transferrin saturation and, in selected cases, soluble transferrin receptor provide additional information.

Is there an optimal ferritin level for runners?

There is no universally validated ferritin concentration guaranteeing optimal running performance. The Australian Institute of Sport (AIS) uses ferritin below 35 µg/L with normal haemoglobin in its athlete-focused framework for iron deficiency without anaemia. This is a management classification—not proof that performance declines at exactly that value or improves when ferritin is pushed above it. [6]

Clinical findings, symptoms, history and other blood measurements matter more than a single supposed performance target.

3. What happens when iron-deficient runners take iron?

Klingshirn and colleagues (1992) randomised 18 iron-depleted, non-anaemic female distance runners to oral iron or placebo for eight weeks. Supplementation improved ferritin, but both groups improved time to exhaustion and the between-group difference was not statistically significant. [1]

This small null trial cannot rule out benefits elsewhere. It illustrates the key distinction: improving ferritin and improving running performance are not necessarily the same outcome.

4. Why another trial found a benefit

Hinton and colleagues (2000) studied 42 iron-depleted, non-anaemic women. Participants received iron or placebo over six weeks, and both groups completed structured aerobic training during the final four weeks. [2]

Both groups improved their 15 km laboratory cycling time trial, but the iron group improved more: approximately 3.4 minutes versus 1.6 minutes. The benefit appeared in how much women improved with training, not in their starting performance.

The limitation is important: participants were previously untrained women, not experienced distance runners, and the outcome was a cycling test rather than a running race. It supports a possible role for iron in aerobic adaptation, but not a forecast of race-time gains for trained runners.

Could baseline functional deficiency explain the difference?

A follow-up analysis by Brownlie and colleagues examined baseline soluble transferrin receptor, a marker relevant to tissue iron demand. Treatment-related training gains were more apparent among women with elevated baseline values than among those with normal values. [3]

This suggests that depleted storage iron and more advanced functional deficiency may not have the same consequences. But the subgroup evidence is limited, drawn from a small research program, and does not validate an individual prediction threshold.

5. What do larger reviews conclude?

The 2025 athlete-focused review

Pengelly and colleagues reviewed 23 studies involving 669 female athletes across 16 sports. The review associated iron deficiency with impaired endurance performance and identified improvements in some supplementation studies. But samples and methods varied, and the narrative synthesis does not supply a reliable percentage improvement for an individual runner. [4]

The 2026 controlled-study meta-analysis

A separate meta-analysis included 15 controlled studies involving 380 women with iron deficiency, with and without anaemia. Iron status improved with supplementation, but separately pooled time-trial and time-to-exhaustion improvements were not statistically significant. Maximal aerobic-capacity findings were more favourable, but less certain when participants with anaemia were excluded. [5]

The reviews considered different populations and types of evidence. The first focused on athletes and included observational evidence; the second pooled controlled interventions from a broader population. They should not be treated as directly interchangeable experiments.

Physiological improvement is also not synonymous with race performance, which depends on running economy, sustainable intensity, pacing and event preparation. The balanced conclusion is that iron deficiency can matter, while treatment-related performance effects remain variable.

6. Does iron deficiency affect training adaptation?

Three outcomes should be kept separate:

  • Iron status: whether blood indicators of deficiency improve
  • Training adaptation: whether training produces physiological capacity gains
  • Performance: whether those gains translate into a better result in a specific event

They are related, but not interchangeable. The Hinton and Brownlie trials suggest functional iron deficiency may alter adaptation in some women; neither establishes that every experienced runner with low ferritin will improve after supplementation. [2] [3]

7. When is iron testing worth considering?

Persistent unexplained fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance or declining performance can warrant assessment for iron deficiency among other potential causes. Relevant risk factors include:

  • Heavy menstrual bleeding or previous iron deficiency
  • Low dietary iron or overall energy intake
  • High endurance-training loads
  • Dietary restrictions affecting iron availability

None diagnoses deficiency. Fatigue and reduced performance are non-specific and may also reflect insufficient recovery, low energy availability, illness or sleep problems.

The AIS recommends a minimum blood panel of ferritin, haemoglobin and transferrin saturation. Additional testing depends on clinical circumstances. [6]

Timing matters

The AIS advises testing preferably in the morning, with adequate hydration, only low-to-moderate activity in the preceding 24 hours and no muscle-damaging exercise (for example, eccentric work) for 2–3 days beforehand. Signs of infection or illness should also be considered. [6]

Testing frequency should reflect individual risk and clinical judgement, not a universal calendar for every runner.

8. Should runners supplement to improve performance?

Iron supplementation treats an identified need; it is not a general performance strategy. Management can include diet, oral supplementation and investigation of contributing factors such as heavy menstrual bleeding or gastrointestinal problems. A qualified clinician should determine treatment and follow-up.

Unnecessary supplementation may cause gastrointestinal effects and excessive iron exposure can be harmful. People with haemochromatosis, an inherited condition associated with iron overload, require particular caution and should not self-prescribe iron. [6]

Intravenous iron carries additional medical and anti-doping considerations. Athletes subject to anti-doping rules should note that IV infusions and/or injections exceeding a total of 100 mL in 12 hours are prohibited outside specified hospital treatment, surgical or clinical diagnostic exceptions, unless an applicable Therapeutic Use Exemption (TUE) permits the procedure. The restriction concerns infusion volume, not iron itself. Check the rules and medical pathway before treatment. [7]

How quickly might performance improve?

There is no reliable timeline for a race-performance improvement. Blood markers may respond before symptoms or training capacity, and studies lasting several weeks do not establish a universal time to benefit. Follow-up blood testing can assess iron status; it cannot quantify how much faster a runner should become.

9. What does this mean for a runner with low ferritin?

Runner A has low ferritin, normal haemoglobin and no unusual symptoms. Clinical review and investigation of potential causes are appropriate, but a predictable performance gain cannot be assumed.

Runner B has low ferritin, persistent fatigue and declining endurance performance. Deficiency may be contributing, especially if additional markers suggest reduced iron availability. Assessment should also consider other explanations.

Runner C has normal haemoglobin but recurrent low iron stores and heavy menstrual bleeding. Normal haemoglobin does not exclude deficiency, and identifying why stores repeatedly fall may matter as much as restoring them.

10. What science still cannot tell us

We lack a validated ferritin-to-race-time relationship, a universal performance-loss threshold and reliable evidence predicting who will improve after treatment. We also cannot assume short interventions in previously untrained women translate directly to highly trained runners.

Future studies need well-characterised trained runners, reproducible running outcomes and longer measures of training adaptation. Until then, ferritin should not be used to prescribe intensity or estimate lost race time.

The bottom line

Iron supports oxygen transport and aerobic metabolism, and deficiency can arise before anaemia. Some controlled trials find performance or adaptation benefits from treatment; others find improved iron markers without improved endurance outcomes.

The evidence supports identifying and appropriately treating deficiency, not supplementing universally or chasing an arbitrary ferritin target.

The goal is not to maximise ferritin. It is to ensure inadequate iron availability is not limiting normal physiological function and the ability to train.
