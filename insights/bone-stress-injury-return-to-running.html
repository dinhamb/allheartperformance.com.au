Bone Stress Injuries in Runners: Why Fitness Can Return Before Bone Is Ready

Running & Injury  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Bone stress injuries are not simply the result of running too much. Understanding training load, bone health and the limits of return-to-running evidence can help athletes make better recovery decisions.

A runner recovering from injury can experience a frustrating mismatch. After several weeks of cycling, swimming or other cross-training, cardiovascular fitness may remain surprisingly good. Breathing feels comfortable, heart rate is under control and the temptation to resume normal running is strong.

But the injured bone may not be ready.

Unlike cardiovascular fitness, bone tolerance to repeated impact cannot be established from an easy aerobic session. A runner can feel capable of completing a session while exposing incompletely recovered bone to inappropriate mechanical loading.

Bone stress injuries have causes extending beyond training volume: energy availability, menstrual function, bone density, previous injury, biomechanics and the characteristics of training load all matter. Research favours a multifactorial approach, yet evidence for specific return-to-running schedules remains limited.

Successful recovery requires more than restoring fitness. It requires restoring the capacity to tolerate running loads.

1. What is a bone stress injury?

Bone is living tissue that adapts to mechanical loading. Running supplies repeated forces that can support skeletal adaptation, but bone remodelling takes time. When cumulative loading exceeds tissue capacity to tolerate and recover from it, microscopic damage can accumulate faster than it is repaired.

Bone stress injuries span early stress-related changes through to visible fracture lines. “Stress reaction” and “stress fracture” describe parts of this spectrum, although imaging terminology varies. The 2025 international Delphi consensus emphasises multifactorial assessment of the injury and athlete. [1]

Why runners are vulnerable

Distance running involves thousands of repetitive loading cycles. The relevant exposure includes duration, frequency, intensity and terrain—not just the force of one footstrike. Running itself is not inherently harmful to bone; problems arise when loading exceeds the skeleton's current capacity to tolerate and adapt. Sudden training changes can contribute, but injuries also develop during stable training when skeletal health or recovery is impaired.

2. Bone stress injury is not just a mileage problem

Two runners might cover identical weekly distances while having very different risk profiles. One has gradually built training, maintains adequate energy intake and has no injury history. Another has recurrent bone stress injuries, irregular periods and periods of inadequate fuelling.

Equal mileage does not mean equal skeletal tolerance. Nor does the second runner's history make reinjury inevitable.

Energy availability and bone health

Problematic low energy availability can disturb endocrine and metabolic processes supporting bone remodelling. Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) is a syndrome associated with problematic low energy availability and possible impairment of skeletal health, reproductive function and other systems. [5]

Menstrual dysfunction can be relevant, but has several causes; regular periods do not guarantee normal bone health. Not every bone stress injury indicates REDs. The REDs in Recreational Runners article explores the physiological and recovery evidence in more detail.

The cumulative risk problem

Barrack and colleagues followed exercising girls and women in a prospective multisite study. Bone stress injury risk increased with accumulating female athlete triad-related risk factors, including inadequate energy availability, menstrual dysfunction and low bone mineral density. Certain combinations of low density, high exercise exposure and dietary restraint carried particularly elevated risk. [2]

The reported risk estimates are specific to that study population, not an individual fracture probability calculator. The practical point is that mechanical exposure and physiological vulnerability can interact.

3. Why injury location and severity matter

Not every bone stress injury carries the same healing or complication risk. Anatomical location helps determine management, protected loading and return-to-sport criteria.

Lower-risk locations

Posteromedial tibial bone stress injuries are generally considered lower risk and may, in appropriate cases, follow a clinically guided progressive loading approach. “Lower risk” does not mean trivial.

Higher-risk locations

High-risk sites include the anterior tibial cortex, femoral neck, navicular and selected proximal fifth metatarsal regions. For the fifth metatarsal, concern especially involves the metaphyseal–diaphyseal junction and proximal diaphysis rather than every tuberosity injury. High- and low-risk injuries should not be managed interchangeably. [1]

Deep hip or groin pain could indicate a femoral neck stress injury. Stop running, avoid putting weight through the affected leg and seek urgent medical assessment.

If a femoral neck fracture progresses or displaces, blood supply to the femoral head can be compromised, potentially causing avascular necrosis and serious joint damage. A generic walk-run program is not appropriate for an undiagnosed or incompletely assessed high-risk injury.

A normal X-ray does not exclude bone stress injury

Early bone stress injuries may be invisible on initial X-rays. When symptoms strongly suggest bone stress injury despite normal or inconclusive radiographs, MRI is generally the preferred next investigation. It can detect stress-related changes and help establish severity. [6]

A normal X-ray is not permission to continue running through focal bone pain.

Why MRI severity matters

Nattiv and colleagues followed 211 collegiate track-and-field and cross-country athletes; 34 sustained 61 bone stress injuries. Higher MRI grade and lower total-body bone mineral density were associated with longer return-to-sport times. Injuries at predominantly trabecular-rich sites, including the femoral neck, pubic bone and sacrum, were associated with longer recovery. Female athletes with oligomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea had higher-grade bone stress injuries than those with regular menstrual cycles. [3]

These findings connect injury severity, menstrual health and skeletal risk without establishing that menstrual dysfunction caused the injuries. MRI grade, bone density and location offer prognostic information, but cannot establish an exact recovery date for an individual runner.

4. Why cardiovascular fitness cannot establish bone readiness

Cycling, swimming and similar rehabilitation activities can preserve aerobic fitness without reproducing running's impact-loading pattern. Strong cycling performance cannot establish that a tibia, metatarsal or femoral neck tolerates repeated running impacts. Conversely, some runners can begin appropriately controlled impact rehabilitation despite detrained cardiovascular fitness.

Three aspects of readiness

Physiological readiness: Is the athlete sufficiently healthy, supported by energy availability, recovery and medical management?

Skeletal readiness: Has the injury healed enough for the proposed mechanical exposure?

Running readiness: Can the athlete progressively tolerate the frequency, duration and intensity required for training?

This is a conceptual framework—not a validated diagnostic score. Success in one domain does not establish success in another.

5. What does research say about returning to running?

George, Sheerin and Reid's 2024 tibial bone stress injury scoping review included 50 publications: 39 reviews or clinical commentaries and only two randomised controlled trials. The authors characterised the recommendations as resting predominantly on Level IV evidence. [4]

Expert reasoning and clinical experience provide useful frameworks, but comparative research has not established a universally superior progression. The review identified pain-free walking, bony tenderness, functional loading, injury severity and contributing factors as recurring considerations, with imaging evidence of healing important for selected high-risk injuries.

Pain-free walking

Ordinary walking and daily activities are commonly expected to be pain-free before running resumes. Published recommendations differ on how long or how far pain-free walking must continue; no universal threshold has been validated.

Localised bony tenderness

Local tenderness is useful, but complete absence of tenderness may not be required in every carefully managed lower-risk tibial injury. Higher-risk injuries warrant stricter criteria, sometimes including complete resolution and imaging evidence of healing. These distinctions require professional assessment.

Strength and functional loading

Clinicians may assess strength, balance, hopping and movement quality. No single test has been validated as a universal guarantee of safe return. Passing a functional test is one input, not immunity from recurrence.

Imaging and healing

Repeat imaging may be required for selected high-risk injuries, while it is not always necessary in lower-risk tibial injuries. Decisions should account for location, severity, clinical progress and the treating team's judgement.

Shared decisions, with clear responsibilities

The review encourages decisions shared between athlete, clinician and coach. Athletes contribute symptoms, goals and preferences; coaches guide progressive training; clinicians determine medical suitability, precautions and investigations. Shared decision-making does not authorise a coach to clear a high-risk fracture independently. [4]

6. How should running loads be reintroduced?

Once a clinician confirms that running can safely resume, exposure is generally reintroduced gradually. Walk-run intervals are common, with short runs interspersed with walking. Running duration or distance is typically increased before speed and intensity, but exact schedules vary and remain poorly validated. [4]

Why a universal progression is problematic

A low-grade posteromedial tibial injury differs greatly from a severe high-risk injury. Progression should reflect:

  • Injury location and imaging severity
  • Clinical recovery and elapsed time
  • Previous running and injury history
  • Bone density and physiological health
  • Energy availability and recovery
  • Symptoms during and after loading
  • Functional tolerance

The familiar 10%-per-week mileage rule is not a validated safeguard against recurrence. It must not override symptoms, individual risk or clinical judgement.

Symptoms during progression

Localised pain during running, worsening symptoms afterwards or deteriorating walking tolerance suggest load may be excessive. Progression may need to pause or be reduced, with reassessment as appropriate. Pain should not be accepted as the price of rebuilding fitness; high-risk injuries need stricter management and must not be progressed through pain via a generic rule.

7. Addressing the causes of the original injury

Rehabilitation is incomplete if it simply pauses training before returning to the workload that preceded injury.

Training exposure

Review volume, frequency, intensity, terrain and recent changes. A clear training error may not exist, and apparent stability does not exclude overload when tissue capacity has changed.

Energy availability and nutrition

Low energy availability can compromise bone health. Clinical and sports-dietetic input may be warranted for suspected under-fuelling or REDs. See the REDs article. Calcium and vitamin D can matter in selected athletes but supplements are not substitutes for resolving underlying causes.

Menstrual and endocrine health

Absent or irregular periods may warrant assessment. Hormonal contraception can obscure spontaneous menstrual function, and withdrawal bleeding does not confirm normal endogenous ovulation.

Bone mineral density

Clinicians may consider DXA in recurrent injuries or where other risk factors exist. In premenopausal women and younger athletes, results are generally interpreted using age- and sex-matched Z-scores. A Z-score at or below −2.0 is generally below the expected range for age. Among women in weight-bearing sports, a score below −1.0 can also raise concern, particularly alongside other female athlete triad risk factors. [7]

These thresholds have different meanings. A Z-score below −1.0 is neither a universal osteoporosis threshold nor proof of REDs. Clinical context and fracture history matter, and DXA alone cannot establish readiness to run.

Strength and biomechanics

Strength, footwear and movement characteristics may be relevant, but not every bone stress injury comes from a particular technique. Interventions should address identified factors, not rely on generic gait corrections.

8. What about female runners specifically?

Bone stress injuries affect runners of all sexes. In some women, menstrual dysfunction, low energy availability and reduced bone density may influence risk. The Barrack and Nattiv studies support looking at these factors alongside training exposure and injury severity. [2] [3]

An injury is not proof of REDs or inadequate fuelling, and normal weight or regular bleeding does not guarantee skeletal health. For the limitations of cycle-based assumptions, see Should Female Runners Train Around Their Menstrual Cycle?

Many studies involve young or collegiate athletes, limiting direct generalisation to adolescents, masters runners and recreational populations.

9. What science still cannot tell us

There is no highly validated individual fracture-risk model based on weekly mileage, intensity, menstrual history and bone density. Aerobic performance cannot establish bone readiness, no universally validated return-to-running program spans all injury sites, and common progression rules lack high-certainty comparative trials.

Clinical consensus is useful but not equivalent to robust intervention evidence. Future studies need prospective comparisons with injury-site classification and underlying skeletal health incorporated.

The bottom line

Bone stress injuries develop when loading exceeds the skeleton's current capacity to tolerate and adapt. Risk and recovery involve injury history, bone health, energy availability, menstrual function and anatomy alongside training load.

A runner can be aerobically fit while the injured bone cannot tolerate running. Return requires clinical evaluation, injury-specific healing considerations and progressive impact tolerance—not just motivation or weeks since diagnosis.

Being fit enough to run is not the same as being ready to tolerate the loading of running.
