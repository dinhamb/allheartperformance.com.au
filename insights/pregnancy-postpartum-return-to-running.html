Running During Pregnancy and After Childbirth: When Is It Safe to Return?

Running & Physiology  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Exercise during pregnancy can provide important health benefits. But returning to running after childbirth involves more than waiting for medical clearance or reaching a particular number of weeks postpartum.

For many runners, running provides routine, fitness, competition, social connection and identity. Some continue running through much of pregnancy; others reduce training or stop because of symptoms, complications or personal preference. Afterwards, the question becomes: when is it safe to start again?

Advice has sometimes centred on a six-week postnatal check. The influential 2019 UK postnatal running guidance proposed waiting approximately 12 weeks before planning a return to running. [8] But neither date demonstrates that an individual runner is ready. Pregnancy and childbirth affect the pelvic floor, abdominal wall, musculoskeletal system and cardiovascular physiology, with recovery varying by delivery, injury and circumstances.

The central question is not simply how many weeks have passed. It is whether a runner is sufficiently recovered to tolerate the activity she wants to perform.

1. Is running safe during pregnancy?

For most women with uncomplicated pregnancies and no contraindications, activity is beneficial. The 2019 Canadian guideline recommends at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity weekly across at least three days, combining aerobic and resistance exercise. Appropriate exercise is associated with a lower likelihood of gestational diabetes and hypertensive disorders. [1]

Continuing established running is different from starting a demanding new program. An experienced runner may continue with modifications, while someone previously inactive should introduce appropriate activity progressively.

What changes during pregnancy?

Blood volume, cardiac output and resting ventilation rise. Body mass and its distribution change, along with abdominal and pelvic tissues. Running mechanics, comfort and perceived effort can shift, so pre-pregnancy pace targets may become less helpful. Perceived exertion, the ability to speak during moderate activity, symptoms and obstetric advice can help guide intensity.

Heat and hydration

Pregnant runners should avoid exercise in excessive heat, particularly high humidity, and maintain adequate hydration. Australian summer conditions may warrant cooler training times, shorter sessions or alternative activities. [1]

When should running stop or be modified?

Certain complications, such as pre-eclampsia, persistent bleeding, premature labour and significant cardiac disease, require individual medical advice. Vaginal bleeding, chest pain, fainting, fluid leakage, significant breathlessness at rest or painful regular contractions require stopping activity and seeking medical assessment. With complicated pregnancies, the treating obstetric clinician should determine suitability.

For uncomplicated pregnancy, the objective is appropriate activity and health, not protecting pre-pregnancy running performance at all costs.

2. What happens to running capacity after childbirth?

Recovery differs following uncomplicated vaginal delivery, caesarean section, significant perineal injury or other complications. Pelvic floor symptoms, abdominal function, pain, lactation, sleep disruption and energy availability all matter.

Cardiovascular readiness and repeated-impact readiness are not interchangeable. A runner can retain considerable aerobic fitness while tissues remain less tolerant of running. Another may be ready for short, easy impact exposures but need months to rebuild endurance training.

Fatigue is not always just sleep deprivation

Sleep disruption is common, but blood loss during childbirth may contribute to postpartum iron deficiency or anaemia. Persistent or disproportionate fatigue deserves evaluation rather than automatic attribution to newborn care. See Low Ferritin and Running Performance for the endurance context.

3. What does the 2025 postpartum guideline recommend?

The 2025 Canadian guideline was developed using systematic evidence reviews and GRADE. For postpartum women without contraindications, it recommends at least 120 minutes per week of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity over four or more days, incorporating aerobic and resistance exercise. This is a general health target, not a prescription for 120 minutes of running. [2]

The guideline also recommends gradual activity progression, pelvic floor muscle training, and attention to sleep, nutrition and mental health.

Does activity need to wait 12 weeks?

No. For medically appropriate cases, the guideline supports beginning or resuming moderate-to-vigorous activity within the first 12 weeks, with light activity such as gentle walking often possible earlier. This does not instruct every woman to start running immediately. Running adds repeated impact loads and needs sufficient healing, including after surgery or perineal injury, without increased vaginal bleeding. [2]

What about the six-week check?

A postnatal review can identify healing and health concerns, but clearance for general exercise does not demonstrate pelvic and lower-limb tolerance of previous running loads. Reaching either six or twelve weeks is not itself a functional readiness test.

A useful screening resource

The guideline includes the Get Active Questionnaire for Postpartum, which can help identify women who may need medical advice before starting or continuing moderate-to-vigorous activity. See the published guideline and associated resources. It is not a validated running-impact clearance test. [2]

4. What does return-to-running research recommend?

Two international Delphi statements published in 2024 summarised expert opinion on readiness and training progression. They address pelvic floor function, incontinence, prolapse symptoms, lower-limb and lumbopelvic capacity, balance, mechanics and individual circumstances. These are expert consensus statements, not large trials defining universally safe numerical thresholds. [3] [4]

What about three weeks?

The readiness consensus suggests a minimum three weeks of relative rest and recovery before considering a gradual, individualised return to running. It is not automatic medical clearance, an instruction to run at three weeks, or evidence that three weeks is safe for everybody. Medical and psychological concerns, current capacity, symptoms and training history must be considered; many runners need longer. [3]

Three different types of readiness

General exercise readiness: Is activity medically appropriate given healing and symptoms?

Impact readiness: Can repeated running impacts be introduced without unacceptable symptoms or complications?

Training readiness: Can the runner gradually tolerate the intended frequency, volume and intensity?

These are conceptual distinctions, not a validated clinical score. Readiness in one domain does not establish readiness in another.

5. Why pelvic floor health matters

The pelvic floor supports continence and pelvic organs. Pregnancy and delivery may affect its tissues and neuromuscular function. Running creates repeated impact and intra-abdominal pressure demands; urinary leakage, pelvic heaviness, prolapse symptoms and coordination difficulties deserve attention.

Is urinary leakage normal?

Urinary leakage is common after childbirth but should not be dismissed as inevitable or untreatable. The 2025 guideline encourages daily pelvic floor muscle training to reduce incontinence risk and rehabilitate pelvic floor function; its formal recommendation is graded strong. Instruction by a qualified pelvic health physiotherapist can help with technique and individualisation. [2]

Symptoms do not automatically require permanent cessation of running. The training consensus notes insufficient evidence to determine whether running with certain pelvic floor symptoms worsens long-term outcomes. That uncertainty calls for individual assessment, not ignoring symptoms or imposing a blanket ban. [4]

What about abdominal separation?

Diastasis recti—separation between rectus abdominis muscles—is common during pregnancy and may persist. Inter-recti distance alone has not been validated as a running-clearance threshold. Symptoms, abdominal function and tolerance of load are more useful in clinical context. [3]

6. Returning to impact loading

Once medical recovery and individual assessment support running, loading is generally rebuilt progressively. The Delphi training consensus supports individualisation, strength work and symptom monitoring, although no specific walk-run interval or weekly increase has been shown superior for everyone. [4]

Build duration before intensity

Short, easy runs separated by walking can help reintroduce impact. Progress running duration before demanding speedwork, hills or large volumes; increasing duration, frequency and speed together makes any emerging symptoms harder to interpret. A runner tolerating short easy sessions may not yet be prepared for previous long runs or hard intervals.

Monitor the response

Assess urinary leakage, pelvic heaviness, musculoskeletal pain, increasing vaginal bleeding and unusual fatigue during and after activity. Worsening symptoms may require reducing or pausing loading and clinical review. Following caesarean delivery or significant perineal injury, healing and specific restrictions take priority. Progressive pain or severe symptoms are not simply an acceptable cost of rebuilding fitness.

7. Strength training has an important role

Running forces travel through the foot, ankle, knee, hip, pelvis and trunk. Pregnancy, decreased training exposure and recovery can alter capacity to manage these loads. The international consensus identifies hip, calf, trunk and pelvic floor strength as relevant considerations. [4]

But it does not prove that one specific strength program prevents injury or accelerates racing return. Some runners need general lower-limb strength; others need pelvic health rehabilitation, trunk work or progressive impact exposure. The program should reflect individual findings, not a universal checklist.

8. Breastfeeding, energy availability and bone health

Lactation adds energy and fluid requirements to daily life and training. Adequate intake and hydration matter, but breastfeeding alone does not establish energy deficiency. An Accredited Sports Dietitian can assist when training volume or fuelling logistics are difficult. [2]

Does exercise affect breast milk?

Appropriate moderate physical activity generally does not harm breast milk supply or quality. Breastfeeding is not itself a general exercise contraindication. Breast comfort, supportive clothing, timing feeds and hydration may help with running logistics. [2]

Bone health during lactation

Pregnancy and lactation involve changes in maternal calcium metabolism and bone turnover. Temporary reductions in bone density may occur during breastfeeding, often with substantial recovery after weaning. These physiological changes do not mean that breastfeeding routinely causes bone stress injury or makes running unsafe. [7]

Focal bone pain, particularly in the hip or groin, deserves medical assessment rather than being dismissed as a normal postpartum change.

What about absent periods while breastfeeding?

Absent menstruation during lactation can be physiological and, by itself, is not a sign of REDs. Where fuelling, fatigue, injury or other symptoms raise concern, consider the broader clinical picture; see REDs in Recreational Runners.

9. Sleep, mental health and training adaptation

Newborn care, disrupted sleep, feeding and family responsibilities change recovery resources. A runner may be aerobically capable of intervals but unable to recover consistently from a demanding schedule. The 2025 guideline recognises mental-health benefits of physical activity, including fewer depressive symptoms, while acknowledging substantial postpartum barriers. [2]

Shorter sessions, less intensity and reduced weekly load may be appropriate. Training should support wellbeing, not become another measure of coping with parenthood.

Where to seek support

Persistent low mood, anxiety or difficulty coping deserve attention independently of performance. A GP can arrange assessment and care. In Australia, PANDA's National Helpline offers perinatal mental-health support on 1300 726 306. Outside its operating hours, Lifeline is available on 13 11 14, 24 hours a day. Call 000 in an immediate emergency.

10. When can a runner return to racing?

Racing brings additional demands beyond comfortable short running sessions. A short road race, marathon and trail ultramarathon differ in duration, intensity and mechanical exposure. Preparation should account for progressive volume, harder workouts, symptoms, sleep and nutrition.

There is no validated universal postpartum timeline for competitive endurance running. Woodroffe and colleagues' review supports individualised, multidisciplinary management of pelvic floor health, nutrition and muscular and skeletal reloading. [6]

Being able to run comfortably is not the same as being prepared to race.

11. What science still cannot tell us

We lack validated functional thresholds that predict safe running return after childbirth, one optimal postpartum week, and strong comparative trials selecting a best walk-run progression. More research must consider delivery type, pelvic symptoms, lactation, sleep and injury outcomes across different runner populations.

Delphi consensus provides expert guidance, not direct proof from large randomised rehabilitation trials. Practical frameworks such as Selman and colleagues' are informative but not universally validated prescriptions. [5] Better prospective studies following runners back to training and competition are needed.

The bottom line

Appropriate physical activity during pregnancy and afterwards offers meaningful health benefits. Running can be part of that activity for many women, but postpartum readiness involves tissue healing, pelvic function, musculoskeletal tolerance, energy availability, sleep and personal context.

The Canadian guideline supports early progressive activity where medically appropriate. Expert return-to-running consensus supports individual assessment and gradual impact reloading. Neither defines a universal week for running, nor treats clearance for general exercise as permission to resume previous loads.

The goal is not to return as quickly as possible. It is to return in a way that supports sustained participation, health and performance.
