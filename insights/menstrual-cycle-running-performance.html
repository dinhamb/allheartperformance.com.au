Should Female Runners Train Around Their Menstrual Cycle? What the Science Actually Shows

Running & Physiology  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Hormones change throughout the menstrual cycle. But does that mean running performance changes predictably, and should training change with it?

The idea is appealing: organise running around the menstrual cycle, match hard sessions to supposedly favourable hormonal phases and schedule recovery when performance is assumed to be reduced. It sounds sophisticated. But it assumes both that reproductive hormones produce predictable performance effects and that phase-based training produces better results. Neither claim follows automatically from hormonal fluctuations.

Oestrogen and progesterone influence exercise physiology, and menstrual symptoms can meaningfully affect training. But the evidence for consistent phase-specific performance advantages remains much less certain.

Current evidence does not support a universal menstrual-cycle training schedule for female runners. The useful alternative is to understand each athlete's actual symptoms, performance and health.

1. What changes during the menstrual cycle?

In naturally occurring cycles, ovarian hormone concentrations change across the follicular phase, ovulation and luteal phase.

Follicular phase

The follicular phase begins with menstruation. Oestradiol and progesterone are typically low early in this phase; oestradiol then rises as the dominant follicle develops.

Ovulation

Ovulation follows a surge in luteinising hormone. Oestradiol is commonly elevated beforehand, while progesterone is relatively low until after ovulation.

Luteal phase

After ovulation, progesterone rises alongside a secondary increase in oestradiol. Both generally decline before menstruation if pregnancy does not occur.

Cycle length and hormone timing vary, and not every apparently regular cycle is ovulatory. Calendar day alone cannot establish an athlete's hormonal state.

What might the hormones influence?

Hormonal changes can influence thermoregulation, ventilation, fluid regulation and metabolism. Resting core temperature is typically higher in the luteal phase, partly due to progesterone. Researchers have also examined muscle function, substrate use, exercise tolerance and recovery.

But an observed physiological mechanism does not establish that a runner will be meaningfully faster or slower—or that training at a particular phase improves long-term adaptation.

2. Does exercise performance change across the menstrual cycle?

McNulty and colleagues' 2020 systematic review identified 78 studies, with 51 contributing to the initial pairwise meta-analysis. On average, performance in the early follicular phase was trivially lower than in other phases: standardised effect approximately −0.06, with a 95% credible interval of −0.16 to +0.04, which includes zero. [1]

The studies covered different sports, participants and tests, with substantial variability and generally low methodological quality. The authors did not support generalised exercise-performance recommendations based on menstrual phase.

What does a trivial effect mean for runners?

It does not mean every woman changes by the same amount, and it cannot be translated into seconds lost over 5 km or a shift in threshold pace. Pooled mixed-sport results test whether a consistent general pattern exists; they are not individual running-performance prediction models.

A small average does not rule out larger individual effects. It means those effects cannot responsibly be assumed from the average or from cycle dates alone.

3. What happens when researchers verify menstrual phases properly?

Research can misclassify phases when it relies on reported period dates or an assumed day-14 ovulation. Without hormone verification, participants assigned to one phase may have different actual hormonal states.

The 2025 hormone-verified review

Schlie and colleagues included only studies measuring oestradiol, progesterone and luteinising hormone to verify cycle phases. Just 19 studies involving 279 participants met those requirements. The average study included about 14 participants, and elite athletes were underrepresented. [2]

Of the included studies, 58% reported a statistically significant phase effect on at least one performance-related outcome. But the direction and magnitude were inconsistent. Maximum and explosive strength were largely unaffected, and important risks of bias remained.

Why 58% does not mean most women are affected

That figure describes studies, not individual women. A study measuring several outcomes has multiple chances to find at least one significant difference. It does not establish that 58% of women experience performance impairment, nor identify a universally superior endurance phase.

More rigorous phase verification narrows the available evidence without revealing a dependable general training rule.

4. Does the cycle affect VO₂max, threshold or running economy?

Distance-running performance depends on maximal aerobic capacity, sustainable intensity, economy and the ability to retain performance after accumulated exercise. Unfortunately, direct running-specific evidence remains limited; much of the literature combines other sports and laboratory tests.

VO₂max and maximal aerobic performance

Some studies report phase differences and others do not. The hormone-verified review identified a small number of less favourable early-follicular VO₂max findings, insufficiently consistent to predict meaningful monthly changes for every runner. [2]

Threshold and sustainable intensity

Hormonal effects on substrate metabolism justify investigating sustained exercise, but no reliable population-wide pattern has been demonstrated for lactate threshold or critical speed. Threshold training should follow demonstrated fitness, training goals, recovery and individual circumstances.

Running economy

Running economy—the energy or oxygen cost of running at a given speed—is crucial for performance. Yet there is no reproducible menstrual-phase pattern supporting automatic changes to running zones or predicted race pace.

Fatigue resistance

Hormonal effects on heat regulation, substrate use and perceived effort make durability an interesting question. But we lack high-quality, running-specific comparisons of fresh capacity, prior exposure and fatigued capacity across verified cycles. Cycle timing is not a validated predictor of late-race endurance performance.

5. Should training be periodised around the cycle?

Acute performance and long-term adaptation are different outcomes. Completing a slightly stronger session on one day does not prove that training on that day produces greater fitness gains. A slightly lower output can still provide a useful stimulus.

What do training studies show?

Most phase-based training research concerns resistance exercise, not endurance running. Some small studies favour particular scheduling strategies, but weaknesses include small samples, uncertain phase verification and differences in training volume.

Colenso-Semple and colleagues' 2023 review concluded that current evidence does not support appreciable menstrual-phase effects on acute strength or resistance-training adaptations, while highlighting underlying methodological limitations. [3]

There are no convincing long-term trials demonstrating greater VO₂max, threshold, economy or race-performance gains from systematically assigning running sessions to menstrual phases.

What do recent consensus statements recommend?

A 2025 international consensus led by Bangsbo and colleagues acknowledged major gaps in knowledge about hormonal effects on performance and adaptation. It cautioned against using currently available menstrual-phase prediction apps to determine training or nutritional decisions based on presumed hormone patterns. [4]

A separate UEFA consensus on menstrual tracking in women's football likewise found the evidence for phase-based performance or injury predictions inconclusive, while recognising the usefulness of tracking symptoms and menstrual irregularities. The guidance concerns football and should not be treated as proof of a running-specific training intervention. [6]

Tracking menstrual health and prescribing workouts from unverified predicted phases are not the same practice.

6. Menstrual symptoms may matter more than phase

Cramping, headaches, gastrointestinal disturbance, fatigue and poor sleep can disrupt training independently of a change in aerobic capacity. Some runners experience debilitating symptoms; others notice little or no difference.

Symptom-informed training

When symptoms repeatedly compromise a demanding session, shifting it, reducing intensity or adjusting the weekly sequence may preserve training consistency. Such changes respond to observed individual constraints, not a claim that everyone performs worse in that phase.

Symptoms deserve treatment, not just scheduling

Severe or persistent menstrual pain and heavy bleeding deserve clinical assessment. Conditions such as endometriosis and adenomyosis may warrant investigation. Heavy bleeding can contribute to iron deficiency; see Low Ferritin and Running Performance.

Absent or persistently irregular periods also warrant assessment, including possible low energy availability and other causes. See REDs in Recreational Runners. Training changes should not replace appropriate healthcare.

7. What about hormonal contraception?

Contraceptive methods alter hormonal physiology differently. Combined oral contraceptives usually suppress ovulation and introduce synthetic hormone exposure; progestogen-only methods and hormonal intrauterine devices cannot be treated as equivalent.

Does the oral contraceptive pill impair performance?

Elliott-Sale and colleagues' 2020 meta-analysis reported generally trivial, variable average exercise-performance differences favouring naturally menstruating women over oral-contraceptive users. Performance appeared relatively consistent across pill-cycle phases, and evidence did not justify general recommendations about contraceptive use for performance. [5]

This is not proof that every contraceptive is performance-neutral. Contraceptive decisions should reflect medical needs, symptoms, preferences and reproductive goals—not a presumed running benefit alone.

Why cycle tracking becomes more complicated

Withdrawal bleeding does not confirm normal spontaneous ovulation, and absent bleeding may be an expected effect of certain methods. Apps assigning natural follicular or luteal phases to contraceptive users may not reflect their actual hormonal state.

Starting the combined oral contraceptive pill solely to produce bleeding is not a treatment for energy-related functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea; it can mask whether spontaneous menstrual function has recovered. See the REDs article for its clinical and bone-health implications.

8. Can runners identify their own patterns?

Symptom tracking can help, but apparent patterns also arise by chance. Training load, sleep, illness, temperature, fuelling and work stress change between sessions. One poor interval workout during menstruation does not establish a hormonal cause; repeated difficulties may reflect symptoms rather than reduced underlying capacity.

A purposeful approach

The Australian Institute of Sport's female-athlete resources encourage monitoring menstrual health and relevant symptoms rather than treating calendar-based predictions as verified hormone measurements. [7]

Runners who find tracking useful can record timing, bleeding characteristics, symptom severity and effects on sleep, recovery and training over several cycles. Three months may provide more context than one isolated session, but sensible symptom-informed adjustments need not wait for a prescribed number of cycles.

Tracking should be voluntary and proportionate to a genuine decision. Ordinary training data should remain the primary basis for fitness progression. Tracking is useful when it informs action, not when it creates another score demanding attention.

9. What should female runners do differently?

Keep proven training principles: progressive overload, recovery, appropriate intensity, fuelling and event-specific preparation.

Adjust for meaningful symptoms: pain or disturbed sleep may justify moving a session, but not every menstrual cycle requires a recovery week.

Separate health concerns from training variation: persistent dysfunction, severe pain and heavy bleeding need clinical assessment.

Avoid automatic phase-based prescriptions: evidence does not justify routinely scheduling VO₂max, threshold and recovery sessions according to predicted cycle days.

Use individual evidence carefully: recurring symptom patterns may support practical coaching decisions without proving a hormone-mediated performance effect.

10. What science still needs to establish

Better studies need trained runners, hormone-verified phases, repeated cycles and performance outcomes relevant to racing. Long-term trials must establish whether changing training distribution by verified phase improves adaptation compared with conventional individualised programs.

Researchers also need to separate hormonal effects from symptoms, sleep, fuelling and other influences. Adolescents, masters athletes experiencing perimenopause and users of different contraceptives remain incompletely represented.

Some women may experience genuine phase-related effects obscured by average results; alternatively, those effects may be smaller and less consistent than popular advice implies. Insufficient evidence for a universal rule is not proof that every individual effect is absent.

The bottom line

The menstrual cycle produces real physiological changes, and symptoms can meaningfully affect training. But current research has not established a dependable universal pattern of endurance performance or superior long-term outcomes from phase-based running schedules.

The best-supported approach is not to train according to a menstrual calendar. It is to train according to the individual athlete, while taking menstrual health and symptoms seriously.
