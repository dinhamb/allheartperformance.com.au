REDs in Recreational Runners: When Training Outpaces Fuelling

Running & Physiology  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Low energy availability can impair endurance performance and physiological health, even in runners who appear fit and healthy. Research also shows why recovery involves more than eating more or getting one period back.

Running improvement depends on a familiar process: apply an appropriate training stimulus, recover from it and gradually develop the capacity to tolerate more work. But recovery requires resources.

A runner might follow a structured training program and maintain a healthy-looking body weight, yet struggle to recover or improve. Sleep, excessive training, illness and individual variation are possible explanations. Another is insufficient energy intake to support both exercise and normal physiological function.

Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) is a syndrome of impaired physiological functioning associated with problematic low energy availability. Its manifestations can affect health, recovery and athletic performance. It is not confined to professional athletes. [1]

Studies of recreational female runners have reported symptoms and behaviours associated with low energy availability, even at body weights within the usual range. These screening findings do not establish clinical diagnoses. [5] [6]

This article focuses on female runners because most recovery experiments discussed here involved women. REDs can also affect men, with possible signs including reduced testosterone and libido. [1]

1. What is low energy availability?

Energy availability is the dietary energy remaining for normal physiological functions after exercise energy expenditure, expressed relative to fat-free mass. It supports tissue repair, bone remodelling, reproduction and metabolic regulation as well as training. [1]

Insufficient energy can result from increasing running volume without increasing food intake, missing meals around training or underestimating the energy cost of exercise. It need not reflect intentional restriction or cause obvious sustained weight loss.

Low energy availability versus REDs

Low energy availability describes an energy balance relationship. REDs is the clinical syndrome that can develop from problematic, prolonged or severe low energy availability. The IOC identifies potential consequences affecting reproductive and bone health, metabolic and endocrine regulation, immunity, mental health and performance. [1]

A brief period of under-fuelling does not necessarily cause REDs; fatigue alone does not establish it. Assessment requires clinical judgement, exclusion of alternative explanations and interpretation of multiple indicators. The IOC REDs CAT2 provides a framework, not an online self-diagnosis. [2]

2. Can inadequate fuelling affect endurance performance?

Caldwell and colleagues (2024) studied 12 endurance-trained women in a randomised, blinded crossover experiment. Participants maintained approximately eight hours of weekly endurance training across two 14-day diet conditions. Mean energy availability was approximately 52 kcal/kg fat-free mass/day in the adequate-energy condition and 22 in the low-energy condition. This was a substantial experimentally imposed deficit. [3]

Performance declined after 14 days

Following low energy availability, 20-minute cycling time-trial performance fell by 7.8%—about 16.8 watts—and remained impaired after three days of restored energy availability. Participants lost approximately 2.6 kg, but the performance reduction persisted when power was expressed relative to body or fat-free mass. [3]

Becoming lighter did not improve relative cycling power in this experiment. But cycling supports body weight; the result must not be translated directly into expected changes in running economy or race times.

What happened to the muscles?

Despite poorer performance, investigators reported no significant changes in skeletal-muscle mitochondrial respiratory capacity, muscle glycogen concentration or maximal activity of several metabolic enzymes. [3]

These null findings do not establish unchanged aerobic fitness or reveal the mechanism of the decline. They do weaken an explanation based solely on depleted resting muscle glycogen. Performance can deteriorate without a detectable reduction in the particular muscle measures tested.

What about immunity and high-intensity exercise?

Jeppesen and colleagues examined the same 12 women and reported altered immune-cell oxidative responses, higher resting cortisol, changes in responses to exercise and impaired high-intensity time-to-exhaustion performance. [4]

These findings suggest multiple systems can respond to severe short-term low energy availability. Caldwell and Jeppesen, however, are companion analyses of the same participants—not independent replications. Nor does a 14-day intervention automatically establish clinical REDs.

3. REDs is not only an elite-athlete problem

Recreational runners balance training with work, study and family demands. For some, meals and recovery become harder to manage as volume increases.

Karlsson and colleagues (2023) surveyed 85 recreationally active female runners aged 18–39. Nineteen per cent screened positive for symptoms associated with low energy availability and 18% for eating-disorder symptoms. Most participants, including many who screened positive, had body mass indices within the conventional normal range. [5]

Dervish and colleagues surveyed 524 competitive and recreational female endurance runners in the UK. Positive screening rates were 47.3% for low energy availability, 40.0% for disordered eating and 9.4% for eating-disorder risk. [6]

These are questionnaire-based screening results, not prevalence estimates for clinically confirmed REDs or eating disorders. The studies demonstrate that potential concerns exist outside elite sport, not how many recreational runners meet a clinical diagnosis.

4. Why REDs can be difficult to recognise

There is no single symptom or routine blood test that reliably diagnoses REDs. Fatigue, poor recovery, declining performance and recurrent illness are non-specific; sleep problems, infection, psychological stress, iron deficiency and excessive training may produce similar patterns. Normal performance does not prove that other physiological systems are functioning normally.

What about screening questionnaires?

The Low Energy Availability in Females Questionnaire (LEAF-Q) was developed to identify athletes at risk of conditions associated with low energy availability and the female athlete triad. Its original validation reported 78% sensitivity and 90% specificity against the study's reference measures. [7]

A 2025 study by Wasserfurth and colleagues compared questionnaire results from 50 German female endurance athletes with a classification based on selected IOC REDs clinical indicators. Fourteen met the study's case definition. LEAF-Q sensitivity was 79%, specificity 50%, positive predictive value 38% and negative predictive value 86%. [8]

Relative to that reference classification, most positive questionnaires did not correspond to a classified case, and some athletes meeting the reference criteria screened negative. The comparison was not against a definitive independent diagnostic gold standard. Screening can prompt assessment, but does not establish or exclude REDs.

Menstrual history adds information, but not certainty

Functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea occurs when hypothalamic hormonal signalling supporting the menstrual cycle is suppressed, sometimes in connection with low energy availability, psychological stress and heavy training. It is a diagnosis of exclusion: pregnancy, thyroid disorders, elevated prolactin, polycystic ovary syndrome and medication effects must be considered. [14]

Hormonal contraception complicates interpretation. Withdrawal bleeding does not confirm normal spontaneous ovulatory function, and absent bleeding with some methods is an expected medication effect. Oral contraceptives can mask whether spontaneous menstruation has resumed, while bone loss may continue if energy deficiency persists. [1] [14]

5. What happens when energy intake increases?

The REFUEL randomised controlled trial studied exercising women with oligomenorrhoea or amenorrhoea. In the menstrual-disturbance comparison, 76 women were randomised to increased energy intake (40) or control (36) for up to 12 months. Dietary intake in the intervention group increased by approximately 330 kcal/day. [9]

Increasing intake improved the likelihood of menstrual bleeding: the intention-to-treat analysis reported a hazard ratio of 1.91 (95% confidence interval 1.27–2.89). This supports a benefit of nutritional intervention in this population, not a universal prescription to add exactly 330 calories per day. [9]

An important limitation of REFUEL

Attrition was substantial. Of the 76 women randomised to increased intake or the menstrual-disturbance control group, only 33 completed the full 12 months: 17 intervention and 16 control. Intention-to-treat analysis helped retain information from participants who did not complete follow-up, but attrition limits precision and generalisability, particularly in smaller secondary analyses. [9]

The overall bone result

In the 2022 bone analysis, greater intake was associated with increases in body mass, body fat and menstrual frequency, but bone mineral density did not significantly improve relative to controls over 12 months. This suggests that reproductive outcomes and skeletal recovery may proceed differently; the trial did not establish a generalised short-term bone-density benefit. [10]

6. Why getting one period back is not complete recovery

A later REFUEL analysis examined consecutive cycles and ovarian hormones. A single return of bleeding did not reliably confirm normal ovarian function; some cycles were anovulatory. Average oestrogen exposure rose significantly—approximately 32%—only among women achieving at least three consecutive cycles shorter than 36 days. Even regular bleeding did not imply ovulation in every cycle. [11]

One period is encouraging but does not establish complete reproductive recovery.

The 2026 bone-recovery findings

A secondary analysis grouped 25 women in REFUEL's increased-intake arm by achieved menstrual recovery. Fourteen experienced at least two consecutive cycles shorter than 36 days; eleven did not. The recovery group showed greater lumbar spine bone-density improvements and favourable bone turnover changes. Differences were not explained by measured energy-intake differences. [12]

The two-cycle bone analysis and three-cycle oestrogen analysis used different outcomes and group definitions; neither number is a clinical recovery threshold.

Why those findings need caution

Women were not randomly assigned to recover or not recover. Another 2026 analysis found that baseline menstrual history and body fat were associated with advanced recovery, so groups may already have differed before the intervention. [13]

The bone study also used last-available DXA measurements obtained at different follow-up points, including months 6, 9 and 12. Its small sample, outcome-defined groups and unequal follow-up limit causal claims. It does not prove that achieving two cycles directly causes improved bone mineral density. [12]

Taken together, the studies suggest that energy intake, menstrual bleeding, ovarian hormone function and bone measures may recover at different rates. Medical and nutrition follow-up may still be appropriate after symptoms or periods improve.

7. What should recreational runners do?

Fuelling and training-load decisions

Consider dietary energy and carbohydrate availability part of training, especially as volume increases. When energy availability is inadequate, management may involve increasing food intake, reducing training load or both. An Accredited Sports Dietitian can help with practical fuelling, restrictive eating and persistent symptoms. [1] [14]

When symptoms warrant assessment

Persistent decline, poor recovery, recurrent illness and menstrual changes warrant a wider health and training review, ideally with a GP or sports physician. Care may involve a sports dietitian and psychologist when appropriate. REDs should not be self-diagnosed using calorie estimates, body weight or screening scores alone. [14]

Menstrual function and bone health

Periods stopping for three months or more, or cycle intervals persistently exceeding 45 days, warrant clinical evaluation. More urgent concerns should not wait for these thresholds. Starting an oral contraceptive pill solely to restore bleeding or improve bone density is not recommended for energy-related amenorrhoea. [14]

A clinician may consider DXA bone-density assessment after six months or more without periods, or earlier with substantial energy deficiency or skeletal fragility. [14]

Localised bone pain that worsens with running, causes limping, or persists when walking or at rest warrants stopping aggravating impact activity and assessment. Deep hip or groin pain can indicate high-risk femoral neck bone stress injury and requires urgent evaluation.

Recovery monitoring and eating-disorder support

Restored training performance or one episode of bleeding does not establish complete recovery of reproductive function or bone health. Follow-up should reflect the clinical findings, especially after prolonged menstrual dysfunction or bone stress injuries.

Low energy availability is often unintentional but can coexist with disordered eating. If restriction or distress around food and body weight is present, discuss it with a GP or qualified professional. In Australia, the Butterfly Foundation National Helpline is available on 1800 33 4673; check its website for current hours and public-holiday arrangements.

8. What the research still cannot tell us

It remains difficult to predict which recreational runners with low energy availability will develop clinical REDs, particularly before substantial health changes appear.

The 12-participant experimental study used a severe controlled deficit and cycling tests; its results cannot be translated into marathon times. REFUEL enrolled women with established menstrual disturbances and had high attrition. The newer recovery-group bone findings remain exploratory.

Prospective research is needed to understand how physiological systems recover, particularly ovarian function and bone health, and whether findings in these selected populations generalise to recreational runners.

The bottom line

Training provides the stimulus for adaptation. Sufficient energy supports the biological processes that make adaptation possible.

Severe short-term low energy availability can impair endurance performance without a detectable reduction in tested mitochondrial measures. Increasing dietary intake can improve menstrual outcomes in some exercising women, but recovery is not one event: restored performance, restored bleeding and restored bone health are not interchangeable.

Normal body weight does not rule out inadequate energy availability, and neither a difficult training block nor a screening questionnaire can establish REDs.

The right response is to recognise persistent concerns, seek appropriate assessment and give fuelling and physiological recovery the same attention as training load.
