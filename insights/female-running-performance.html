Female Running Performance: Iron, Energy Availability and the Menstrual Cycle

Running & Physiology  ·  October 14, 2026

By Braden Dinham — All Heart Performance

Running performance is often explained through aerobic fitness, training volume, intensity, running economy and recovery. Improve those variables, and performance should improve. But that explanation is incomplete.

Two runners can follow similar training programs, accumulate comparable workloads and appear equally committed, yet experience very different outcomes. One adapts consistently. The other struggles with persistent fatigue, declining performance or recurrent injuries.

Sometimes the difference is training history, recovery or normal individual variation. Other times, important physiological or health factors have been overlooked.

For female runners, iron status, energy availability, menstrual health and bone health deserve particular attention. These factors do not affect every woman in the same way, and many are also relevant to male athletes. But they can meaningfully influence the ability to tolerate training, recover and perform.

The important question is not whether women need an entirely different training system. It is whether the assumptions behind the training program remain valid for the individual runner.

1. Low energy availability: when training exceeds the body's available resources

Endurance training creates a significant energy demand. Running requires energy not only to complete each session, but also to recover, repair tissue and support normal physiological functions.

Energy availability describes the dietary energy remaining for these functions after exercise energy expenditure has been accounted for, relative to fat-free mass.

When energy availability is persistently or substantially insufficient, physiological adaptations may conserve energy at the expense of other functions. However, the relationship between measured energy availability and clinical consequences varies between individuals. There is no single universally reliable threshold at which Relative Energy Deficiency in Sport (REDs) develops.

Low energy availability is not necessarily intentional. A runner might increase training volume without sufficiently increasing dietary intake, struggle to eat around early-morning sessions, underestimate training energy demands or repeatedly complete demanding sessions without adequate fuelling. It can also occur in athletes whose body weight appears stable.

What is REDs?

REDs describes a syndrome of impaired physiological functioning associated with problematic low energy availability. Its potential consequences extend well beyond running performance.

The International Olympic Committee's 2023 consensus statement identifies effects involving reproductive function, bone health, metabolic regulation, immune function and other physiological systems. For runners, the implications may include impaired recovery, menstrual dysfunction and greater susceptibility to bone stress injury. [1]

Not every short period of insufficient fuelling causes REDs. Nor does every athlete with fatigue or menstrual symptoms have the condition. REDs is a clinical syndrome, not another name for feeling tired or under-fuelled after a long run.

Why body weight can be misleading

Body weight alone cannot establish whether an athlete has adequate energy availability. A runner may maintain weight while experiencing changes in physiological function. Conversely, low body weight or recent weight loss does not independently establish REDs.

Accurately measuring energy availability outside controlled research settings is difficult. The IOC REDs Clinical Assessment Tool, Version 2 (CAT2), therefore considers multiple clinical indicators and uses risk stratification rather than a single body-weight value, questionnaire or laboratory result. [2]

Practical implication: When a runner develops persistent fatigue, declining performance, recurrent injury or menstrual changes, fuelling and energy availability deserve consideration before additional training stress is prescribed. Assessment may involve a sports dietitian and a clinician familiar with REDs.

2. Iron deficiency: an overlooked constraint on endurance performance

Iron is essential for endurance exercise. It is a core component of haemoglobin, which carries oxygen in the blood, and myoglobin, which supports oxygen storage and availability within muscle. Iron is also involved in enzymes and mitochondrial processes essential to aerobic energy production.

Female endurance athletes can be vulnerable to iron deficiency because of menstrual blood loss, low dietary iron intake, gastrointestinal losses and exercise-associated changes in iron regulation. Heavy menstrual bleeding can substantially increase iron requirements.

Iron deficiency can occur without anaemia

Iron-deficiency anaemia develops when iron deficiency progresses sufficiently to impair haemoglobin production. But iron stores can become depleted before haemoglobin falls below the normal range. This is known as iron deficiency without anaemia.

A runner may therefore have normal haemoglobin while still having depleted iron stores. Ferritin, a protein associated with iron storage, is commonly used to assess those stores, but it is not a direct measurement of endurance capacity.

Ferritin is also affected by inflammation, recent strenuous exercise and muscle damage. Where practical, blood collection should be standardised and scheduled away from demanding or muscle-damaging training, following clinical guidance. Assessment may include a full blood count, ferritin, transferrin saturation and, where appropriate, inflammatory markers.

Does correcting iron deficiency improve running performance?

A 2025 systematic review by Pengelly and colleagues examined 23 studies involving 669 female athletes across multiple sports. The authors reported associations between iron deficiency and impaired endurance performance, with improvements in some supplementation studies. Many of the underlying studies were small, and participants, interventions and outcomes varied. [3]

A separate 2026 meta-analysis examined 15 controlled studies involving 380 women with iron deficiency, including participants with and without anaemia. Unlike Pengelly's athlete-focused review, it was not restricted to competitive athletes. [4]

Iron supplementation improved iron-status markers, including ferritin and haemoglobin, but pooled time-trial and time-to-exhaustion changes were not statistically significant when analysed separately. Measures of maximal aerobic capacity improved, although that finding became less certain after excluding participants with anaemia.

These results do not mean iron deficiency is unimportant. They show that correcting a physiological deficiency and producing a predictable improvement in race performance are different outcomes. The evidence does not justify promising that raising ferritin by a particular amount will improve a 5 km or marathon time by a particular percentage.

Unnecessary or incorrectly used iron supplements can cause adverse effects. Investigation and treatment should be guided by a healthcare professional, not by an arbitrary performance-oriented ferritin target.

When should a runner consider iron assessment?

Persistent unexplained fatigue, reduced exercise tolerance, unusually difficult sessions or declining performance can justify considering iron deficiency among several explanations. Heavy menstrual bleeding, previous deficiency, dietary restrictions and substantial endurance-training demands strengthen the case for investigation.

Heavy menstrual bleeding also deserves assessment in its own right, particularly when it interferes with daily life, causes dizziness or is associated with recurrent deficiency.

These symptoms are not specific to iron deficiency. The objective is to identify a possible constraint—not diagnose iron deficiency from training data.

3. The menstrual cycle: what changes, and what actually affects performance?

The menstrual cycle involves changes in reproductive hormones, particularly oestrogen and progesterone. Progesterone contributes to the rise in resting core temperature commonly observed during the luteal phase. Hormonal changes may also influence ventilation, fluid regulation and other aspects of exercise physiology.

Symptoms such as cramps, headaches, gastrointestinal discomfort, migraines and disturbed sleep can alter the experience of training. But a measurable physiological change is not necessarily a meaningful, predictable change in running performance.

What does the research show?

A 2020 systematic review and meta-analysis by McNulty and colleagues examined 78 studies. Average performance appeared trivially lower during the early follicular phase than during other phases, with substantial variability and important methodological limitations. The authors did not support generalised menstrual-phase training prescriptions. [5]

A 2025 review by Schlie and colleagues required menstrual phase to be verified using measurements of oestradiol, progesterone and luteinising hormone. Only 19 studies involving 279 participants met those requirements. Some found phase-related differences, but the direction and magnitude were inconsistent; elite athletes were underrepresented. [6]

There is therefore insufficient evidence to establish that every female runner should schedule intervals, threshold sessions, strength training or recovery weeks around a universal menstrual-cycle template.

Why cycle-based training is appealing

If hormones change predictably across a cycle, a correspondingly predictable training schedule sounds reasonable. The problem is that the proposed performance benefit has not been demonstrated reliably. Hormonal responses vary, cycles differ, and outcomes depend on training workload, recovery, nutrition, sleep and training history.

A plausible mechanism does not establish that a particular training schedule improves performance.

Individual symptoms still matter

The absence of a reliable population-level performance pattern does not diminish severe menstrual pain, heavy bleeding, migraines or sleep disturbance. These can substantially disrupt training quality independently of any proposed hormone-driven performance advantage.

Tracking symptoms alongside training can identify practical constraints, but a single difficult session does not demonstrate a hormonal pattern, and repeated observations do not establish causation.

The evidence supports individualisation—not universal menstrual-phase training rules.

4. Menstrual dysfunction is different from normal performance variation

Menstrual symptoms and menstrual dysfunction are not interchangeable. Cramps during menstruation do not necessarily indicate abnormal reproductive function.

By contrast, if previously regular cycles become persistently irregular, or periods stop without an obvious explanation, clinical investigation is warranted.

Low energy availability is one possible cause, but pregnancy, thyroid disorders, polycystic ovary syndrome, medications and other endocrine or medical conditions can also affect menstrual function. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises assessment when menstruation stops for three months or more without explanation. [7]

Missing periods should not be accepted automatically as a normal consequence of serious endurance training.

What about hormonal contraception?

Different hormonal contraceptives alter reproductive hormones and bleeding patterns in different ways. Withdrawal bleeding with some methods does not confirm normal spontaneous menstrual function. Conversely, absent bleeding with certain methods may be an expected medication effect rather than evidence of low energy availability.

Research in naturally menstruating participants cannot automatically be applied to those using hormonal contraception. Decisions about contraception should reflect overall health needs and preferences, not performance optimisation alone.

5. Bone health: when an adaptation problem becomes an injury problem

Running repeatedly loads bone. Under appropriate conditions, bone remodels and adapts, provided there is sufficient recovery and physiological support.

Problematic low energy availability and associated endocrine changes may compromise bone health, increasing the risk of bone stress injury. Such injuries arise when cumulative loading exceeds bone's capacity to tolerate and adapt to stress, on a spectrum from stress reaction to stress fracture. [1] [8]

Training progression, biomechanics, previous injury, bone density, fuelling and menstrual health can influence risk. No single factor explains every injury.

Warning signs should change the decision

Localised bone pain during running—especially pain that progressively worsens, causes limping or persists while walking or at rest—should prompt stopping aggravating impact activity and seeking clinical assessment.

Pain around the hip or groin may indicate a high-risk femoral neck injury and warrants urgent evaluation. Other high-risk sites include the anterior tibial cortex, navicular and proximal fifth metatarsal, particularly the metaphyseal–diaphyseal junction. [8]

Repeated bone stress injuries, persistent menstrual dysfunction and a history of restrictive fuelling warrant consideration of underlying physiological problems. The goal is not only to treat the current injury, but to identify constraints on recovery and adaptation.

6. When should a runner take action?

The central challenge is distinguishing expected training fatigue from patterns requiring a different response.

Ordinary fatigue after demanding trainingReview load, recovery and fuelling
Persistent unexplained fatigue or declineReview context; consider assessment including iron and energy availability
Recurring menstrual symptoms disrupting sessionsIndividualise scheduling and symptom management
Periods stop or become persistently irregularSeek clinical evaluation
Heavy menstrual bleedingSeek assessment, including consideration of iron deficiency
Recurrent bone stress injuryInvestigate load, mechanics, nutrition and health factors
Localised bone pain, especially hip or groinStop aggravating impact; seek prompt assessment, urgently for suspected femoral neck injury

These observations are not diagnoses. They are reasons to consider whether the explanation extends beyond training load alone.

7. What should coaches and runners do differently?

Female runners do not need entirely separate training principles. Progressive overload, adequate recovery, aerobic development, strength training and event-specific preparation remain fundamental.

Consider fuelling part of the training system. Sufficient total energy and appropriate carbohydrate availability support training and recovery, particularly as duration and intensity rise.

Pay attention to menstrual health without overinterpreting it. Recurring symptoms may justify adjustments; cycle day alone is not a validated readiness marker.

Do not turn blood markers into performance targets. Ferritin and haemoglobin are clinical measurements, not race-time predictors. Suspected deficiency calls for investigation, not routine supplementation.

Protect bone health as well as fitness. Repeated bone stress injury calls for consideration of load, mechanics, nutritional support and underlying health.

Distinguish normal fatigue from persistent dysfunction. A hard training block causes fatigue; sustained deterioration, repeated recovery difficulties or unexplained symptoms merit broader assessment.

8. Putting the evidence into practice

Runner A: declining performance

A half-marathon runner's threshold sessions become unusually difficult despite consistent training. Possible causes include accumulated fatigue, insufficient recovery, inadequate fuelling, iron deficiency, illness or another condition. Simply prescribing more threshold work is premature. Training history, nutrition, sleep and symptoms should be reviewed, with clinical investigation when indicated.

Runner B: predictable menstrual symptoms

A runner experiences severe cramps and poor sleep during the first days of menstruation. Rather than automatically reducing training for an entire phase, her coach reschedules selected sessions around documented symptoms. The adjustment responds to a real individual constraint—not a universal hormonal template.

Runner C: recurrent bone stress injuries and absent periods

A runner has repeated bone stress injuries and previously regular periods have stopped. This is no longer merely a training optimisation question. Medical assessment, including potential low energy availability and other causes of menstrual dysfunction, takes priority over further performance-oriented loading.

The same training response can have multiple explanations. Effective decisions depend on distinguishing ordinary variation from constraints requiring a different approach.

9. What we still do not know

We do not have reliable evidence that assigning endurance-training intensity to particular menstrual phases improves long-term running adaptation across female athletes. Ferritin alone cannot accurately predict individual race performance, and REDs cannot be diagnosed from a weight trend, menstrual log, training-load history or one questionnaire.

Many studies have small samples, short observation periods or participants unlike recreational and competitive age-group runners. Adolescents, masters athletes and users of different hormonal contraceptives remain underrepresented in parts of the evidence.

These gaps do not imply female physiology is irrelevant. They mean we should avoid claiming more certainty than research supports.

The bottom line

Female running performance is not governed by a separate set of training principles. But training does not occur in isolation from physiology and health.

Iron deficiency can constrain endurance function. Problematic low energy availability can compromise multiple systems. Menstrual dysfunction and recurrent bone stress injuries may indicate problems requiring clinical attention. None of this justifies universal menstrual-phase training schedules or treating isolated blood markers as race-time predictors.

A training plan creates the opportunity for adaptation. The athlete's physiological health and available resources help determine whether that adaptation can occur.

Recognising when those conditions are compromised may be more valuable than adding another workout.
